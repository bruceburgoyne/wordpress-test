Most rebrands start with a feeling. The website looks tired, a competitor has refreshed, or a new leader wants to mark a change. None of those are bad reasons to look at your brand, but none of them tell you what the work should achieve. Before anyone opens a design file, it is worth answering a few plain questions as a team.

1. What has changed?

A brand should change when the organisation behind it has changed: new services, new audiences, a merger, a sharper purpose. If the business is the same and only the look feels dated, a refresh may be all you need. Be honest about which one you are in.

2. What do people misunderstand about you?

Talk to clients, staff and people who chose not to work with you. The gap between how you describe yourselves and how others describe you is where a rebrand earns its keep. If there is no gap, the problem is probably elsewhere.

3. What must stay?

Every established brand has things people recognise and trust. A colour, a name, a way of speaking. List them before you start, and decide deliberately which you will keep. Throwing away recognition you have built over years is expensive.

4. Who needs to be involved, and when?

Rebrands stall when the wrong people see the work too late. Agree early who decides, who advises and who simply needs to be kept informed.

  • Decides: a small group, ideally no more than three people.
  • Advises: people who will live with the brand every day, such as sales and service teams.
  • Informed: everyone else, at clear points in the project.

5. How will you know it has worked?

Pick two or three signs of success before you begin. They might be the kinds of enquiries you receive, how easily new staff explain what you do, or whether clients start using your language back to you. Without them, the only measure left is whether people like the logo.

If you can answer these five questions clearly, you are ready to brief the work. If you cannot, that is the first piece of work to do.
